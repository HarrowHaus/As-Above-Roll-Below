# REF-0003 — Talking Board / Ouija

**Status:** verified historical substrate; commercial-name use requires later legal review.

## Canonical subject
Late-19th-century American **talking boards**, including the board marketed as **Ouija**.

## Domain
Spiritualism / commercial game history / popular occult culture.

## Provenance tags
- DOCUMENTED
- HISTORICAL INTERPRETATION for some origin stories
- FOLKLORE for supernatural claims

## Rights / sensitivity
- Historical patents are factual/public records.
- `Ouija` has longstanding commercial brand significance; do not assume unrestricted title/product use in a commercial game merely because the history is old.
- Safer generic game terminology: **talking board** unless legal review approves a specific branded reference.

---

## What is documented

U.S. Patent 446,054, granted February 10, 1891 to Elijah J. Bond and assigned to Charles Kennard and William H. A. Maupin, describes a `toy or game` designated in the specification as an **Ouija or Egyptian luck-board**. The device uses a board of letters/numerals and a movable table/pointer operated by hand contact.

U.S. Patent 462,819, granted November 10, 1891 to Charles W. Kennard, describes a **talking-board** and explicitly references Bond's earlier patent.

Smithsonian's history places the commercial talking board within the much larger late-19th-century American Spiritualist culture and traces the Kennard Novelty Company's role in manufacturing and marketing the product.

## What is claimed / folklore

Claims that boards communicate with spirits, demons, the dead, hidden knowledge, or autonomous entities belong to religious/paranormal belief and folklore, not established mechanical facts about the commercial device.

The origin story in which the board itself supplied the name `Ouija` is part of the product's reported history; treat it as an attributed story rather than a laboratory finding.

## Common errors to avoid

- `Ouija` is not securely established as a simple portmanteau of French *oui* + German *ja*; Smithsonian specifically discusses that popular explanation as contrary to the origin account preserved by historians of the board.
- Do not treat all spirit boards across cultures and periods as one identical tradition.
- Do not claim the patent office certified supernatural operation. The patents establish a device/game, not paranormal efficacy.

## Visual / mechanical hooks

### POINTER
A talking-board artifact naturally suggests selecting among visible outcomes rather than rerolling them.

### LETTER / NUMBER GRID
A mechanical effect could care about exact die faces, sequences, or `spelled` combinations without copying a modern branded board design.

### GOODBYE
A consumable or event can end an encounter early at a cost, using generic talking-board vocabulary only if cleared.

## Comedy hooks

The historical collision is already funny enough: a wildly consequential occult reputation grew around a product whose patent language describes a **toy or game**. That contrast should be treated with total seriousness rather than a `haha demons` gag.
