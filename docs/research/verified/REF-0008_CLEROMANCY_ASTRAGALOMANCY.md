# REF-0008 — Cleromancy / Astragalomancy

**Status:** verified historical-practice substrate; highly relevant to core game cosmology.

## Canonical subject
**Cleromancy** — divination by casting/drawing lots — and **astragalomancy**, divinatory use of astragali / knucklebones and related dice-like objects.

## Domain
History of divination / ancient religion / games of chance / material culture.

## Provenance tags
- DOCUMENTED historical practice/material culture
- HISTORICAL INTERPRETATION for the purpose of individual archaeological objects
- FICTIONALIZED when converted into Below mechanics

## Rights / sensitivity
Ancient practice is broad historical substrate. Do not flatten every culture's lot-divination practice into one universal ritual system.

---

## What is documented

Oxford University Press scholarship on ancient Greek lot practices discusses **cleromancy / lot oracles** as one end of a spectrum ranging from explicit divination to ordinary institutional drawing of lots.

Cambridge scholarship on ancient Greek technical divination notes that both regular six-sided dice and irregular four-sided knucklebones (**astragaloi**) were used in divinatory contexts in the ancient Greek and Roman worlds.

The British Museum catalogs an agate classical-world astragalus as a **gaming piece or for fortune telling**.

The Metropolitan Museum of Art holds Hellenistic glass astragali and explains their use in games of chance. The Met also holds an inscribed twenty-sided die from Egypt, ca. 2nd century BCE–4th century CE, for which divination is described as the most likely purpose; the museum notes ancient oracle procedures in which thrown lots/numbers could lead to prepared questions or responses.

## What this does not establish

- There was no single universal ancient `dice religion`.
- A specific archaeological die cannot automatically be assigned a divinatory purpose unless the context/source supports it.
- Modern tabletop dice mechanics are not direct continuations of one ancient ritual system.

---

## Why this matters to As Above, Roll Below

This gives the game a real historical bridge between **chance, lots, interpretation, and attempts to obtain information about uncertain outcomes**.

The game's fictional system can therefore use `casting`, `lots`, and outcome-fixing language without pretending the fantasy mechanic is a historical reconstruction.

### FICTIONAL TRANSFORMATION

In the Below, local outcomes do not always resolve cleanly. Delvers use a standardized **cast** to expose the competing possibilities.

A die that becomes **locked** is a possibility that has been fixed into the current clash.

The two dice committed to Fight are possibilities the delver **spends on survival / force**.

The two left uncommitted remain available to stabilize as **Spoils** if the clash is won.

This explains the core mechanic diegetically:

> The high die you use to survive cannot also become your treasure because you already spent that outcome making yourself survive.

Whether casting *causes* the settlement or merely *reveals* it remains intentionally unresolved inside the fiction.

## Comedy hooks

The game can treat dungeon dice with absurd ritual and bureaucratic seriousness because history already contains sophisticated systems for interpreting lots and chance.

The joke is not `ancient people thought dice were magic`.

The joke is that our dungeon professionals have developed an absolutely rigid field doctrine around a problem that still fundamentally ends with somebody throwing objects and seeing what comes up.
